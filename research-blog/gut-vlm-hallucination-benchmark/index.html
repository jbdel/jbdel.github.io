Gut-VLM, 7 in 10 GPT-4o endoscopy descriptions had an error

Gut-VLM had gastroenterologists check, sentence by sentence, what GPT-4o wrote about 1,816 endoscopy images, and released the corrections. This post reads what the release holds, and what the paper’s “hallucination-aware” fine-tuning result does and doesn’t show.

Paper Bidur Khanal, Sandesh Pokhrel, … Yash R. Shrestha, Binod Bhattarai. Hallucination-Aware Multimodal Benchmark for Gastrointestinal Image Analysis with Large Vision-Language Models.     TL;DR
    • The release is the contribution. For 1,816 still images of the public Kvasir-v2 set: what GPT-4o wrote, which sentences four gastroenterologists tagged as hallucinated, and their corrected text. It is on GitHub as JSON; the repository has no licence file.
    • GPT-4o needed fixing most of the time. The experts found no error in 552 descriptions and at least one in 1,264. In the mixed ones, 1,739 of 4,670 sentences (37%) were tagged.
    • The headline comparison isn’t like for like. Models fine-tuned to detect and correct hallucinations score 88.4 to 90.9 on the question metric; models fine-tuned to write the description score 82.9 to 83.7. The paper’s figure gives the correcting model GPT-4o’s description as input, and that text alone already scores 86.0%.
    • What can’t be told. How much of the gain is the training recipe and how much is the head start: there is one score per model, no interval, and no baseline that hands the same draft to a differently trained model.

    01What the experiment compares

    Public image-text pairs are scarce in GI endoscopy; the authors cite one earlier set, Kvasir-VQA, with short answers. They build longer ones cheaply: GPT-4o describes each image, then gastroenterologists correct the description instead of writing one from nothing. The corrections leave a trace that ordinary datasets don’t have: which sentences the model got wrong.

    A “report” here is a short paragraph about one still image, not the report of a procedure. Four open models (LLaVA-1.6-7B, DeepSeek-7B-VL, Qwen2-7B and mPLUG-Owl-2B, as the paper names them) are then tested three ways. The two fine-tuned versions share images and LoRA settings (rank 8, five epochs); they differ in the task, and with it in what the model is handed:

    What does the correcting model see at test time? our observation

    The paper doesn’t say; we searched the full text, the reviews and the rebuttal. The repository answers for one model: in the released test outputs of the correcting Qwen, every prompt reads “Can you please correct any hallucinated sentences and generate a modified response?”, and 143 of the 366 answers repeat the expert-corrected reference word for word our measurement, which only a model handed the text can do. The four models’ scores fit: the correcting models’ word overlap with the reference (ROUGE-L 0.85 to 0.89) matches that of GPT-4o’s unedited text (0.87), while the models that write from the image reach 0.50 to 0.55. We read the “hallucination-aware” rows as GPT-4o’s draft after the model’s pass.

    02What GPT-4o got wrong

    So 1,264 descriptions, 69.6% derived, carried at least one tagged sentence. A mixed description has about 3.8 sentences, 1.4 of them tagged derived. The tags mark whole sentences, not the wrong words, a limit the authors name.

    The paper’s Fig. 1 plots GPT-4o’s accuracy by aspect over the whole dataset: about 50% on the anatomical class (which part of the gut the image shows), against about 82% to 91% on colour, position, findings and polyp count, as measured on the chart our measurement. By that chart, GPT-4o got the anatomical class wrong about every other time.

    The set is small and uneven: 1,450 images for training and 366 for testing, and 57% of the 1,816 show a normal landmark. Esophagitis, the smallest class, has 70 images, so 14 test images derived. All come from Kvasir, collected in one Norwegian health trust.

    03What the models score

    The paper’s main metric, QAAS, asks GPT-4o to pull 12 answers out of a generated description and counts how many match the 12 reference answers (Section 04).

    1. Fine-tuning works. As released, LLaVA-1.6-7B scores 50.9% on QAAS; fine-tuned to write the description from the image, 83.1%; fine-tuned to correct, 90.9%. The other three models follow the same order.
    2. The draft is a strong baseline that the paper lists but doesn’t compare against. GPT-4o’s unedited descriptions score 86.0%, which the authors call expected, partly because the references are corrected versions of them. All four writing models sit below that (82.9 to 83.7); all four correcting models sit above it (88.4 to 90.9).
    3. The gain splits in two. Hallucination-aware fine-tuning beats standard fine-tuning by 5.0 to 7.8 points, the paper’s result. Measured from the draft instead, the correcting models add 2.4 to 4.9 points derived.

    On that reading, a small open model trained on 1,450 examples removes, net, between 17% and 35% of the wrong answers in a GPT-4o draft derived: a useful checker on top of a large drafter. That is a different claim from “hallucination-aware beats standard fine-tuning”, which needs both models to start from the same input.

    Where correcting doesn’t beat writing our measurement

    The paper’s Fig. 3 splits QAAS by aspect for three models. Measured on the chart (about ±1 point; the draft’s values are from Fig. 1, over the whole dataset): on colour, where the draft is right about 85% of the time, the correcting models reach about 87 to 88 and the writing models about 63 to 64. On the anatomical class, where the draft is right about half the time, the correcting models reach about 80, 72 and 84, against 82, 84 and 83 for the writing models. A reviewer asked why; the authors suggested the correcting model becomes overly cautious, with no experiment to confirm it. Another reading fits both rows: the correcting model inherits the draft’s answers and has to overrule the wrong ones. It beats the writing model where the draft is mostly right, and not where the draft is wrong half the time.

    04Reading the two new metrics

    QAAS, in the released scripts, takes two GPT-4o calls per description and one set comparison per answer. GPT-4o answers 12 questions from the text in one or two words (“N/A” if the text is silent); GPT-4o maps each answer to a category from a fixed list (for colour: red, pink, orange…; for the landmark: colon, cecum, pylorus, Z-line); an answer is correct when its categories overlap the reference’s. Staying silent where the reference is silent counts as correct.

    R-Sim is one GPT-4o call: rate from 1 to 5 how well two descriptions match, with the 12 questions as a guide. Three cautions:

    • The two metrics disagree about the draft. GPT-4o’s unedited descriptions get an R-Sim of 2.97, below every fine-tuned row, yet a higher QAAS than every writing model. And on DeepSeek, R-Sim goes down with hallucination-aware training, 3.76 to 3.63, while QAAS goes up.
    • GPT-4o drafts, extracts and judges. A reviewer raised it; the authors agreed that several independent judges could be more robust. The paper reports that the extraction step itself errs 4.29% of the time on the reference texts, which were then fixed by hand; model outputs go through the same extractor unchecked, which the authors expect to affect all models alike.
    • No intervals. One QAAS value rests on at most 366 × 12 = 4,392 answers. If answers were independent, a score near 88% would carry about ±1 point and a difference of two scores ±1.4 (unpaired; pairing on the shared images should tighten it); if the 12 answers of an image moved together, ±3.3 and ±4.7 derived. The 5.0 to 7.8 gaps clear both; of the four against the draft, only the largest clears the second.

    One expert also rated 30 sampled LLaVA descriptions per version on a 1-to-5 scale: 1.90, 3.24 and 3.36. The 0.12 between the two fine-tuned versions is within the rater’s own repeat spread: the paper gives a coefficient of variation of 11.41%, about 0.37 per score at that level, so about ±0.19 on a difference between two 30-description averages from rater noise alone derived.

    05What the paper leaves out

    • The test-time input of the correcting model, and a control for it: a writing model given the same draft, or the correcting model given the standard model’s own output.
    • Other ways to reduce hallucination. A reviewer asked for a comparison; the authors acknowledged the gap and deferred it to a journal version.
    • Agreement between the four experts, and how many of them saw each description.
    • The exact checkpoints. Model names are short, and two of the four citations are to papers on other models (the Qwen3 and DeepSeek-V3 language models).
    • Training code and weights. Training ran on the ms-swift toolkit; the repository links no configuration file and no trained weights.
    • The question-answering row. LLaVA fine-tuned to answer the 12 questions directly scores 87.9% on QAAS, above the writing version and the draft; the paper doesn’t set it against either.

    06Takeaways

    1. Score the draft before you score the corrector. Here the draft is worth 86.0% on the paper’s own metric; a corrector’s 90.9% is +4.9 over it, not +40 over the model as released.
    2. Keep the corrections when experts fix LLM drafts. Sentence-level tags cost little extra and turn a caption set into training data for a checker. Expect real work: 7 drafts in 10 needed a change.
    3. Check the anatomical site separately. It is where the draft was weakest, about one in two, and where correcting did no better than writing.
    4. Don’t let the drafter be the only judge. Two GPT-4o-based scores rank GPT-4o’s own text in opposite ways.

    07What you can use today

    • The annotations. In dataset/Gut-VLM of the repository: all_annotations.json (2.2 MB; draft, sentence tags and corrected text for the complete dataset, per the README), VQA_format_all_annotations.json (3.0 MB; the 12 answers), and train_test_split/ with train.json, test.json, question-answer versions and one file per class. training_style/hallucinated_aware_train.json (3.0 MB) is described as the detect-and-correct training file. We read the listing and the README, not the files.
    • The images are not in the repository. Download kvasir-v2.zip (2.3 GB) from Simula’s Kvasir page.
    • The licence. The repository has no licence file, so the annotations come with no stated reuse terms: ask the authors before redistributing. For the images, Simula’s page restricts Kvasir to research and education and forbids commercial use without written permission.
    • The evaluation scripts. eval_scripts/ holds the QAAS chain (caption2vqa.py, parse_vqa.py, evaluate_vqa.py) and R-Sim (evaluate_caption.py). They call gpt-4o through the OpenAI API, so scores can drift as that model changes. The prompts and synonym lists are in the scripts.
    • The rule. When a model’s job is to fix another model’s text, report three numbers: the text before, the text after, and what a model without the draft achieves.
